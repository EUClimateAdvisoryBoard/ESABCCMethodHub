## Implications

1. **The past pace is not a guide to the future, because a large part of it came from producing less.** About 8 of the 19 Mt a
   year came with lower output (E2), and two-thirds of the steel and cement cut did (E3). The net-zero pathways keep output
   roughly stable (E6). The cut from cleaner production therefore has to rise from about 10 Mt a year to 18–28 Mt a year to
   2040, a two- to three-fold acceleration (E5). Read on headline pace alone (19 vs 18–28), the task looks achievable. Seen by
   source, it is a step change.
2. **The easiest "cleaner" gain is already used up.** N₂O abatement in nitric and adipic acid provided −39 Mt (E4), about a
   fifth of all past intensity gains, at low cost. Most remaining industry emissions come from fuel combustion and process CO₂
   in steel, cement, lime and basic chemicals. Cutting them needs new production routes: hydrogen or scrap-based steel, CO₂
   capture for cement and lime, electrified heat, low-carbon feedstocks. These need large investment, infrastructure (hydrogen,
   CO₂ transport and storage, grids) and long lead times. Policy attention should shift from monitoring efficiency to the
   deployment rate of these routes.
3. **Watch output and trade alongside emissions.** There are two readings of the output decline, and both deserve weight:
   - **Leakage and competitiveness:** EU heavy-industry output fell, and production may have moved abroad. Then part of the
     EU cut was relocated, not removed, and continued output loss would raise strategic and employment concerns.
   - **Demand and efficiency:** part of the decline reflects structural demand change, more recycling and material
     efficiency, which lower emissions legitimately and should be encouraged.

   The data here cannot tell these apart. A progress assessment should therefore report physical output and net imports of
   steel, cement and basic chemicals next to emissions, for example from Eurostat Comext and producer statistics. Instruments
   that address leakage (CBAM alongside the ETS) and those that support domestic low-carbon production have to work together,
   because the pathways assume output stays in the EU.
4. **Measure progress in physical units where possible.** Value-based production indices understate the fall in basic-materials
   output (E7). That makes past cuts look more like "cleaner production" than they were. The EU inventory activity data
   (crude steel, clinker, lime, glass, ammonia, ethylene, primary aluminium) give a consistent physical basis. Progress
   indicators for industry should be emissions per tonne for these products, alongside total emissions.
5. **The pathways disagree on industrial output; this is a policy choice, not a modelling detail.** REMIND-EU lets steel and
   cement output decline somewhat, while JRC CETO holds steel stable and raises cement output (E6). Both reach net zero, but
   with different implications for investment needs, trade exposure and employment. The Board could say explicitly which
   output assumption its advice rests on, and test its findings against both.
6. **2040–2050 is about residual emissions and removals, not decomposition.** After 2040 industry emissions in the pathways
   are near zero or negative, through CO₂ capture and bioenergy with CCS. The required 7–20 Mt a year therefore depends on
   CO₂ storage availability and the bioenergy limits the Board has set, not on output or efficiency.

## Limits

- **Coverage of the scenario evidence (E6):** only 3 of 6 ECL pathways report output, from two model families, and only for
  steel and cement. It is evidence, not a set-wide result.
- **Measurement and base year (E2):** the share of the past cut from lower output depends on how output is measured and on the
  base year: one-third to one-half, or 58 % from 2007. The direction of the implications holds across that range; the exact
  share does not.
- **Emissions per unit of output** (the "cleaner production" part) is a residual. It mixes technology, fuel switch, product mix
  within a branch and accounting changes.
- **Leakage:** none of this evidence measures leakage directly (implication 3).
