# The 46% electrification target: cost, feasibility and benchmark

*ETS Review submodule · ESABCC Secretariat · drafted 2026-07-20*

On **17 July 2026** the Commission published its **Electrification Action Plan**
(COM(2026) 595; press release IP/26/1596) alongside the ETS review package. Its
headline is an **indicative target of a 46% electricity share of final energy
consumption by 2040** — roughly double today's stalled ~23%. The target is
*indicative for now*: the Commission intends to propose enshrining it in EU law
via the post-2030 Energy Union package in **Q4 2026**, replacing the existing
KPI of **32% by 2030**. Reaching it, the Commission argues, would cut the EU
fossil-fuel import bill by **~€260 bn/year by 2040**. This note answers four
questions the Board asked about that number:

1. How expensive is 46% electrification **without** demand-side measures versus
   **with** them?
2. What are those demand-side measures?
3. How much of final demand can even be electrified — and is 46% *direct*
   electrification only, or does it include *indirect* electrification via
   hydrogen/e-fuels?
4. Against the 2040 (–90%) modelling, is 46% high or low?

---

## Bottom line

- **46% is a *direct*-electrification target** — electricity's share of final
  energy consumption. Indirect electrification (hydrogen and e-fuels made from
  renewable power) is **on top** and adds roughly another 10–20 points of final
  energy in 2040 scenarios. So the "fully electron-derived" share of 2040 final
  demand is closer to **~55–70%**, not 46%.
- **46% is at the *low* end of the 90%-by-2040 range.** The Board's own 2040
  advice scenarios reach **50–54% by 2040** (61–63% by 2050); the Commission's
  own 2040 impact assessment models **~47–50%**. 46% is the floor, not a
  stretch — ambitious only against the near-stagnant historical trend.
- **The physical ceiling for *direct* electrification is ~60%** of final energy
  with mature/near-term technology (Eurelectric). The residual ~40% —
  high-temperature industrial heat, chemical/steel feedstocks, aviation,
  shipping, heavy freight — needs hydrogen, e-fuels and sustainable biomass.
  46% by 2040 therefore uses **roughly three-quarters** of the realistic
  direct-electrification headroom.
- **Cost — the key policy point:** reaching 46% on **carbon price alone** would
  require a buildings-and-transport price *far* above the ETS2's own €45/t
  price-stability trigger. Published modelling puts price-only at **175–350 €/t
  for the ETS2/ESR sectors** (Ariadne/PIK) and the Board's own stylised run at
  ~€150–200/t; a €45/t price makes heat pumps uncompetitive and delivers only
  3–7 Mt of abatement. **With a demand-side package the same target clears at
  roughly €55–60/t** [modelled]. The gap — on the order of **€100+/t or more** —
  is the shadow value of demand-side policy. Price-only is not just expensive,
  it is *institutionally foreclosed*: the ETS2 releases extra allowances above
  €45/t, so it assumes flanking measures by design.

---

## 1. What "46%" measures — direct vs indirect electrification

The **electrification rate** is a specific, standard indicator: **electricity as
a share of final energy consumption**. The Board tracks it as **Indicator E5 —
"electrification rate (share of electricity in final energy use)."** It counts
electricity delivered to end-uses (an EV charging, a heat pump running, an
electric arc furnace). It does **not** count:

- **Indirect electrification** — hydrogen, ammonia, e-methanol, e-kerosene and
  other synthetic fuels produced *from* renewable electricity and then burned in
  hard-to-electrify end-uses. These are "electrons in disguise" but show up in
  the statistics as hydrogen/synthetic-fuel demand, not electricity.
- Ambient heat captured by heat pumps, waste heat, or district heat.

So **46% is a direct-electrification target.** This matters because the two
levers are complementary, not competing. The Board's 2040 advice is explicit:

> *"All scenarios, even those with a very high electrification rate, require
> alternative non-fossil energy carriers for applications that are hard to
> electrify, for example in industry and transport."*
> — ESABCC, *2040 climate target advice* (2023), p. 17

In the Board's 36 filtered 2040 scenarios, **hydrogen alone reaches 6–23% of
industrial final energy** on top of 33–61% direct electrification. Adding
indirect electrification, the total electron-derived share of 2040 final demand
is materially higher than the 46% headline — roughly **55–70%** across the
scenario set.

**Watch the perimeter.** Electrification shares move 5–15 points depending on
whether international aviation/shipping and non-energy feedstocks sit in the
denominator. On the tighter EED final-energy perimeter numbers read higher; add
international transport and feedstocks and they fall. Much of the apparent gap
between "46%", "47%" and "50%" across studies is definitional, not real
ambition — always check the denominator before comparing.

## 2. How much of final demand can be electrified?

### The direct-electrification ceiling is ~60%

The canonical engineering answer is **Eurelectric, *Decarbonisation Pathways*
(2018)**: to cut EU energy-related emissions ~95% by 2050, **direct
electrification must supply close to 60% of final energy consumption**, with
scenarios spanning **38–60%**. 60% is the deep-electrification variant that is
technically feasible with existing and near-term technology.

Bottom-up sectoral technical potentials (Rosenow & Eyre, *Reinventing energy
efficiency for net zero*, 2022) are higher still where the physics allows:

| Sector | Technical potential for direct electrification |
|--------|------------------------------------------------|
| Buildings | **~97%** (heat pumps; caveated for dense urban housing) |
| Industry | **~76%** |
| Transport | **~54%** (road electrifies; aviation/shipping largely do not) |

Ember (2026) frames the same point from today's baseline: with ~23% electrified
now, **"two-thirds of remaining energy use could be converted using mature
technologies"** — above all road transport and heating.

### The hard-to-electrify residual (~40%) needs hydrogen, e-fuels, biomass

Consistent across the literature, the fraction that resists *direct*
electrification is: **high-temperature industrial process heat, chemical and
steel feedstocks, aviation, shipping, and some heavy freight.** These are the
demand base for hydrogen, e-fuels/synthetic fuels and sustainably-sourced
biomass — and biomass is itself capped by land constraints (~2,400 TWh
sustainable EU supply per ESABCC).

### The Board's sectoral 2040 numbers

From the 36 filtered ESABCC 2040 scenarios (all consistent with a –90%/–95%
pathway):

| Sector | Electricity share of the sector's final energy by 2040 |
|--------|--------------------------------------------------------|
| Buildings (residential + commercial) | **53–71%** (up to 63% in the high-renewables pathway) |
| Industry | **33–61%** (+ 6–23% hydrogen) |
| Whole economy | **≥42%** across all filtered scenarios; **50–54%** in the central advice scenarios |

**Answer to the feasibility question:** ~60% of *final* demand is the realistic
direct-electrification ceiling; ~two-thirds of *remaining* (non-electrified)
demand is convertible with mature tech. 46% by 2040 uses roughly three-quarters
of that headroom and leaves the genuinely hard ~40% to hydrogen/e-fuels/biomass.
The 46% is direct-only; indirect electrification is additive.

## 3. Is 46% high or low for a 2040 pathway?

**Low — it is essentially the floor of the decarbonisation-consistent range.**

The Board's own indicator E5 is the cleanest benchmark. The electrification rate
has been **flat at ~21–23% since 2005** (Eurostat energy balances). Against that:

| Series | 2030 | 2040 | 2050 | Source |
|--------|------|------|------|--------|
| Historic (Eurostat) | ~23% (2021) | — | — | ESABCC E5 |
| Fit-for-55 / CTP MIX benchmark | 30.4% | — | 48.0% | EC (FF55 MIX; CTP MIX, SWD(2020) 176) |
| **ESABCC 2040 advice scenarios** | 29–30% | **50–54%** | 61–63% | ESABCC (2023) |
| **Commission target (Electrification Action Plan)** | — | **46%** | — | EC (17 Jul 2026) |

Two things fall out:

- **46% is below the Board's advised 50–54% for 2040** — 4 to 8 points under the
  low end of the science-based range for a 90% target.
- **46% is even below the Commission's *own* modelling.** Its 2040 impact
  assessment (PRIMES) projects **~47% (EED perimeter) / ~50% (headline)** by
  2040 and **>60% by 2050**; REMIND (PIK) gives 51% in 2040; CLEVER 51%; SP90
  57%. Only the least-electrification-intensive scenarios (Agora "Breaking free
  from fossil gas", TYNDP24 Distributed Energy) land at 46%.

Harmonised comparison of 90%-by-2040 scenarios (négaWatt, *Make electrification
"Fit for 90"*, Jan 2026), electricity as % of final energy in 2040:

| Scenario | 2040 electrification |
|----------|----------------------|
| PAC 2.0 (CAN Europe) | 70% |
| SP90 (Strategic Perspectives) | 57% |
| CLEVER / REMIND (PIK) | 51% |
| EC Impact Assessment (S2/S3) | 47% |
| **Electrification Action Plan target** | **46%** |
| Agora / TYNDP24-DE | 46% |

**Verdict:** doubling from ~23% to 46% is a sharp break with a two-decade
plateau, so it is ambitious *relative to the past*. But measured against the
2040 modelling — including the Commission's own — **46% is the conservative
anchor, not a stretch.** The Commission pitched its indicative *target* at the
very bottom of what its *own models* produce. The Board's line writes itself:
the target is consistent with –90% only if electrification then re-accelerates
hard after 2040, and it leaves no margin for the demand-growth surprises (data
centres/AI add ~100–300 TWh by 2040) that inflate the *ratio* without adding any
decarbonisation.

## 4. Cost: price-only versus with demand-side measures

**The headline gap.** Reaching a 46% economy-wide electrification rate by 2040
through **a uniform carbon price alone** requires a price on buildings-and-
transport fuels *far* above the level the ETS2 was designed to tolerate. **With
a demand-side package** the same physical outcome clears at a small fraction of
that price. In the Secretariat's stylised 2040 sector-coupling run:

| Path | Carbon price (ETS1+ETS2) needed for 46% by 2040 |
|------|-------------------------------------------------|
| Carbon price **only** (no new demand-side measures) | **~€150–200/t** [modelled; central ~€165–170/t] |
| Carbon price **+ demand-side package** (removes ~⅔ of non-price barriers) | **~€55–60/t** [modelled] |
| Baseline drift, no measures | reaches only ~26% [modelled] |

The **~€100+/t gap between the two curves is the shadow price of demand-side
policy** — the money the carbon market has to spend to brute-force through
barriers (split incentives, capital constraints, slow stock turnover,
hassle/search costs) that a well-designed regulation or subsidy removes directly.

**Why price-only is not merely expensive but institutionally foreclosed.** ETS2
— the new carbon market for buildings and road-transport fuels, now delayed to
become fully operational in **2028** (postponed from 2027 in November 2025) —
has a price-stability mechanism: if the allowance price exceeds **€45/t in 2020
prices (~€55/t in 2025 prices)**, additional allowances are released from the
reserve to pull the price back down. A price-only path to 46% needs a
buildings-and-transport price **several times that trigger**, so the mechanism
would fire long before the price could do the electrification work. **The ETS2
architecture itself assumes flanking measures** — it is not designed to be the
sole instrument, and cannot be.

This is not a hypothetical, and the modelled figures above sit *inside* the
published literature. The **Ariadne/PIK** work finds that if CO₂ pricing is the
**only** instrument, hitting the 2030 targets needs **175–350 €/t for the
ESR/ETS2 sectors** (buildings and transport) — versus 130–210 €/t for the
industrial ETS1. Günther, Pahle et al. (*Climate Policy*, 2025, PRIMES) put
ETS2 at **71–261 €/t in 2030** depending purely on how strong the *complementary*
energy-efficiency policies are — the "weak companion policy" case reaches
261 €/t — and report the wider published range at **51–391 €/t**, converging
toward ~475 €/t long-term. Crucially, the same work shows a **€45/t price
delivers only ~3–7 Mt of CO₂ reduction and is "insufficient to make heat pumps
competitive even in the operation phase in several countries"** — so, in their
words, *"a strong policy mix is needed in addition to carbon pricing."*

| Benchmark (2030 unless noted) | Carbon price | Source |
|-------------------------------|--------------|--------|
| ETS2 price-stability trigger (MSR2 release) | **€45/t (2020 prices ≈ €55/t 2025)** | Art. 30h, Dir. (EU) 2023/959 |
| Commission impact assessment (MIX / MIX-CP) | ~48 / up to 80 €/t | EC SWD(2024) |
| ETS2, price + strong companion policy | ~71 €/t | Günther/Pahle 2025 |
| ETS2, price + **weak** companion policy | ~261 €/t | Günther/Pahle 2025 |
| **Price-only, ESR/ETS2 sectors (buildings+transport)** | **175–350 €/t** | Ariadne/PIK |
| Published literature envelope | 51–391 €/t | Günther/Pahle 2025 |

The €45 trigger is not a soft ceiling either: to smooth the ETS2 launch, the
Council and Parliament agreed in 2026 to *double* the volume released at €45
(from 20 to 40 million allowances). So the system is being actively tuned to
keep the price near that trigger — which is exactly why a price-only path to
46% electrification (needing multiples of €45) is foreclosed by design. The
€100+/t gap between the two curves in the modelled table *is* the shadow value
of the flanking measures that close it — consistent with the Edenhofer/Kalkuhl
second-best argument for policy *mixes* over price-only (see §5).

> **Caveat on the modelled figures.** The €150–200/t and €55–60/t come from a
> single-snapshot 2040 LP the Secretariat built for this question, not from a
> published IAM. It has no hourly dispatch (so no heat-pump peak-load feedback
> onto power prices, which would raise both curves somewhat), collapses ETS1 and
> ETS2 into one uniform price, and calibrates barrier costs to the hidden-cost
> literature rather than estimating them. Treat the *gap* (~€100/t) and the
> *ordering* (price-only ≫ trigger ≫ price-with-measures) as robust; treat the
> absolute levels as indicative, pending reconciliation against PIK/Ariadne and
> Öko-Institut published ETS2 price ranges.

## 5. The demand-side measures — what they are and what they fix

### Why a mix, not just a price (the economics)

A single carbon price is first-best *only* if the carbon externality is the
sole market failure. Buildings and transport carry **additional, independent
failures** that a price cannot reach: **split incentives** (the landlord buys
the boiler, the tenant pays the gas bill — Melvin 2018), **slow capital-stock
turnover / lock-in** (boilers and buildings last decades — Seto et al. 2016),
**credit constraints** (heat pumps are a large up-front cost for
liquidity-constrained households), **information/hassle costs** (the
"efficiency gap" — Allcott & Greenstone 2012; Jaffe & Stavins 1994) and **near-
zero short-run price elasticity** of heating and mobility fuel demand. The
Tinbergen rule follows: ≥1 instrument per independent failure. Formal
second-best analysis (Kalkuhl, Edenhofer & Lessmann 2013) and the acceptability
literature (Klenert et al., *Nature Climate Change*, 2018; Pahle et al. 2018 on
"sequencing") both conclude a **carbon price + standards + targeted subsidies +
infrastructure** mix is welfare-superior *and* meets the target at a far lower
price. The measures are ranked below by **which barrier they remove per euro** —
that, not the tonne price, is what lets the target clear at ~€55/t instead of
€175–350/t.

### 1 — Electricity-price reform (the cheapest lever, and the Plan's Pillar 1)

Move network charges, taxes and levies *off* electricity so the
**electricity-to-gas retail price ratio** falls. With a heat-pump seasonal
performance factor of ~3, the heat pump only beats a gas boiler on running cost
when that ratio is **below ~3, comfortably below ~2.5**. Today the ratio sits at
~2.5–3.5 across much of Europe, and **only Finland and Sweden are below 2**; in
most member states electricity is taxed *more* than gas. The effect on uptake is
large and direct: the Netherlands (ratio ~1.4) sells roughly **3× the heat pumps
per 1,000 households** of Germany/Poland/Hungary (ratio >3) (JRC, *Heat Pumps in
the EU*, JRC144191). This one reform shifts the supply curve left for buildings,
transport *and* industry at once — more than any single subsidy. It is the
Action Plan's **Pillar 1** ("future-proof electricity bills," COM(2026) 600):
network-charge reform, an Energy Taxation Directive fix so electricity is not
taxed above gas, and a **KPI capping national ratios at ≤2.5 for households and
≤2 for industry by 2030**.

### 2 — Buildings: boiler phase-out + retrofit support + Social Climate Fund

Regulation with a date resolves what no plausible price can. The **EPBD recast
(Directive (EU) 2024/1275)** already **ends financial incentives for stand-alone
fossil-fuel boilers from 1 Jan 2025 (Art. 17(15))**, requires national plans
toward a **complete fossil-boiler phase-out ~2040**, and mandates zero-emission
new buildings (public 2028, all 2030). National bans (Germany's GEG 65%-
renewable rule; Norway, Denmark, Netherlands) point the same way.

> **Evidence caveat — bans need the price ratio to bite.** In 2024, EU heat-pump
> sales *fell ~22%* (Germany −48%, France −24%) *despite* renewable-heating
> mandates, because subsidy instability and cheap gas dominated (EHPA 2025). The
> lesson is not that regulation fails but that it works **in combination with**
> a favourable electricity/gas ratio (§1) and stable subsidies — the Nordics,
> with low ratios and long-standing bans, reach 85–97% heat-pump shares.
> One-stop-shop retrofit support helps with hassle costs but its quantified
> impact on renovation *rates* is a genuine evidence gap (JRC; Pardalis 2025).

The **Social Climate Fund** (Reg. (EU) 2023/955: €65bn EU / ~€86.7bn with
co-financing, 2026–2032) does the distributional work — funding the up-front
capital vulnerable households can't finance and removing the equity constraint
that would otherwise force a low price cap. Revenue recycling is what makes a
credible carbon price politically durable (Klenert et al. 2018).

### 3 — Transport: fleet CO₂ standards + charging (AFIR)

Cheap per tonne because standards act through the ~15-year vehicle stock-turnover
directly, while the carbon price only reaches drivers via fuel — where short-run
elasticity is very low (≈ −0.02 to −0.34; a fuel tax cuts car CO₂ by only ~1–3%,
so a prohibitive tax would be needed). **Regulation (EU) 2023/851** sets
**−55% (cars) / −50% (vans) by 2030 and 100% (zero-emission) from 2035**;
standards, not price, are the operative driver of the EU's BEV ramp (BEVs ~17%
of the market in 2025 — T&E, ICCT). **AFIR (Reg. (EU) 2023/1804)** guarantees the
charging floor (1.3 kW public per BEV; ≥150 kW every 60 km on TEN-T) so
availability doesn't become the bottleneck — though in the EU charging is
currently running *ahead* of the required trajectory, confirming the vehicle
standard is the binding lever. *(Watch: the Dec-2025 Automotive Package proposes
softening 2035 to a 90% cut with e-fuel/PHEV flexibilities — a proposal, not yet
law, that would dilute the zero-emission signal.)*

### 4 — Industry: CCfDs for electrified heat + fast grid connections

The weakest *electrification* lever per TWh, but the one that unblocks
first-of-a-kind investment. **Carbon Contracts for Difference** guarantee a
strike carbon price over ~15 years, hedging the political and market risk that
stops firms taking the final investment decision on electrified process heat and
H₂-DRI steel (Richstein & Neuhoff, *iScience*, 2022). Germany's
Klimaschutzverträge show the template: Round 1 (Oct 2024) contracted 15 projects
for up to **€2.8bn**, abating ~**17 Mt CO₂e over 15 years**; Round 2 runs to
**€5bn**. CCfDs *complement* rather than replace the ETS, and this is where
**Industrial Decarbonisation Bank** money should land. The binding physical
constraint is grid access: the EU Grid Action Plan (COM(2023) 757) puts grid
need at **€584bn this decade**, with **>3,000 GW of projects (IEA) — and >500 GW
of wind (ACER) — stuck in connection queues**. Fast, maturity-based connection
reform is the enabler electrified industry actually waits on.

The logic is consistent throughout: the carbon price is a blunt, universal
signal; demand-side measures are scalpels that remove the *specific* non-price
barrier in each sector — which is why a modest price plus targeted measures
beats a very high price alone, both in the Board's stylised run and across the
Ariadne/PIK and Commission modelling.
